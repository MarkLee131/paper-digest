## Executive Summary

Thirteen papers through screening, and the argument they make together is about **validation** — specifically,
about what happens when the thing being validated is generated by a language model and the oracle was designed
for code written by a human.

Three papers, arrived independently, attack the same oracle from three sides. An LLM repair agent that patches
a memory-corruption bug is normally accepted if the proof-of-concept stops crashing. **CodeMechanic** names that
oracle weak and shows the failure mode: a patch can silence the observed crash by changing behaviour that has
nothing to do with the bug. Its response is not a better oracle but a *smaller hypothesis space* — reconstruct
the violated memory-safety property from the crash, validate the pointer and its buffer range, insert a local
fail-stop guard, and deliberately trade availability for security while a human prepares the real fix. On 101
real-world ARVO bugs that yields **47.6 % more plausible patches than the best baseline using 91 % fewer
tokens**, and a manual audit finds **3.4×–4.3× more patches semantically equivalent to developer-written
repairs**. A COLM paper on **red-teaming APR agents** comes at the same joint from the security side, asking
what a patch that passes its tests has actually established. And **EAHC**, on Verilog generation, dissects the
two candidate oracles directly: execution signals are deterministic but limited by testbench coverage, LLM-as-a-Judge
is semantically rich but hallucinates on execution-equivalent code. The paper's sharpest empirical point is
negative — letting the reasoner *see* the execution results merely anchors its judgement to the test outcome,
collapsing two signals into one. So the two are acquired independently and fused only at the decision stage.

Against that, the most useful result of the window is a paper that discovered its own scaffolding was
unnecessary. **Risse and Böhme** set out to beat SZZ — the 2005 bug-introducing-commit heuristic whose authors
have just taken the 2026 SIGSOFT Impact Award, and whose successors have moved F1 by only 5–10 points in
twenty years. Their SZZ-Agent, a two-stage pipeline of SZZ candidate filtering plus agentic binary search over
file history, reaches **0.77 F1 on the Linux dataset against 0.64 for the 2025 state of the art**. Then the
ablation labelled, in the paper's own heading, the most surprising finding: raising the binary-search threshold
to infinity — that is, **deleting the binary search entirely** — *improves* F1 to 0.82 and cuts cost per fix
commit from $0.67 to $0.33. The second half of the paper is consequently an argument against its own first
half. Hand a stock coding agent the commit history and ordinary developer tools — read, grep, glob — and it
scores **0.81 / 0.75 / 0.75 / 0.65** across four datasets, beating the elaborate pipeline on three of them. The
mean agent run uses 9.8 reads and 2.7 greps, and grep patterns are drawn from the fix's removed lines (48.1 %)
and commit message (43.6 %) — the agent reinvents SZZ's own move as a search string, then goes further,
because unlike SZZ it can handle a fix that only *adds* lines.

Read next to last window's finding — that a model is most useful translating an artefact you already own —
this window sharpens it. It is not only that you should not ask the model to invent the artefact. You should
also be careful about building machinery around it. Two of the strongest results here were obtained by
*removing* structure: EAHC by refusing to let one signal see the other, SZZ-Agent by deleting its own search
strategy. The pipeline was the thing standing in the way.

Four further candidates were screened out at Stage-1 as saturated or insufficiently novel; they are not named.

**LLM-Based Agents for Identifying Bug-Introducing Commits**

**Authors:** Niklas Risse, Marcel Böhme (MPI-SP and CISPA, Germany)

**Venue:** ASE '26 — Proceedings of the 41st IEEE/ACM International Conference on Automated Software Engineering, Munich, Germany, 12–16 October 2026, 12 pages. Received 2026-03-26; accepted 2026-06-18.

**Links.** [ACM DOI 10.1145/3832783.3834337](https://doi.org/10.1145/3832783.3834337)
· [author-hosted PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ASE26-llm4bic.pdf)
· [artifact, Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19336271).
**License: CC BY 4.0** — the front matter states the work is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution
4.0 International License, copyright held by the authors. **Full text read (12 pp., all tables and appendices).**

### The problem, stated as a stagnation

Identifying which commit introduced a bug determines which released versions are affected — a question with
direct security consequences. SZZ has answered it since 2005, and its authors have just received the 2026 ACM
SIGSOFT Impact Award for it. The paper's framing is that this is precisely the problem: on the
developer-annotated Linux dataset of Lyu et al., the original SZZ scores **0.54 F1**, and the 2025 state of
the art (LLM4SZZ, ISSTA '25) scores **0.64** — ten points in twenty years. Across all five datasets used here
the cumulative gain over original SZZ stays within **5–10 percentage points**.

The diagnosis is structural, not parametric. SZZ assumes the lines *deleted* by a fix are the lines that
introduced the bug. The paper's counterexample is clean: a commit adds a function that fails to validate its
inputs; the fix *adds* a validation check rather than touching the original function. The deleted lines are
then unrelated to the real culprit — and in the limiting case, a fix with zero deletions gives SZZ nothing to
trace at all. Refactoring noise compounds it: prior work (RA-SZZ) attributes **6.5 %** of incorrect
bug-introducing flags to behaviour-preserving changes.

The task is defined in prose, not in a numbered environment: given a fix commit $c_{\text{fix}}$, identify the
set of bug-introducing commits $B \subseteq H$ where $H$ is the prior history; $|B|$ is usually one but need
not be.

### Two tools, and the second one refutes the first

**SZZ-Agent** is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 runs standard SZZ to produce candidates, then hands the agent
the fix message, the fix diff, and each candidate's message and diff with five lines of context, asking for
the earliest responsible commit or an explicit abstention. Stage 2 fires when Stage 1 abstains or SZZ produced
nothing: binary search over the timestamp-ordered history of every file the fix touched, the agent judging at
each step whether the bug is already present, switching to direct selection once the window narrows past a
tunable threshold.

**Simple-SZZ-Agent** is what the ablations produced. No SZZ filtering, no binary search, no stages: collect the
commit histories of all files the fix modified into one candidate set, hand it to the agent together with the
fix message and diff, and give it ordinary developer tools — file reading, grep, glob.

Leakage controls are unusually thorough and worth copying: ground-truth commit hashes replaced with
`COMMIT_HASH` throughout diffs and messages, `Fixes:` lines removed entirely, the agent given a working
directory of loose files rather than a real git repository, and internet access plus the WebSearch/WebFetch
tools disabled.

Evaluation metric, **Equation (1) of the paper** — the only numbered equation it contains:

$$\text{Precision}(c_{\text{fix}}) = \frac{|B \cap \hat{B}|}{|\hat{B}|}, \qquad \text{Recall}(c_{\text{fix}}) = \frac{|B \cap \hat{B}|}{|B|}$$

macro-averaged across fix commits, with F1 the harmonic mean of the averaged values. *(The paper contains no
numbered definitions, algorithms, theorems or lemmas — verified against the full text.)*

### What the numbers say

**RQ1, F1 against eight baselines.** SZZ-Agent scores **0.77 / 0.75 / 0.67 / 0.60** on DS_LINUX, DS_GITHUB-c,
DS_GITHUB-j and DS_APACHE, against LLM4SZZ's 0.64 / 0.63 / 0.55 / 0.51 — gains of **13, 12, 12 and 9 points**.
Significant at $p<0.01$ with large effect sizes, with one honestly-reported exception where the effect size is
large but $p = 0.013$.

**Leakage ruled out empirically.** A new dataset, DS_LINUX-26, built from fixes published between 1 September
2025 and 31 January 2026 — after the backbone model's stated August 2025 cutoff. SZZ-Agent scores **0.81**
there, *higher* than on the older dataset. Baselines move by a point or two.

**The backbone is not the story.** Given the same claude-opus-4-5, LLM4SZZ reaches 0.65 and SZZ-Agent 0.77.
The architecture, not the model, carries the 12 points.

**Context ablation.** Neither message nor diff: **0.48**, below plain SZZ. Diff only: 0.73. Message only:
0.77. Both: 0.81. The commit message alone outperforms the diff alone — a result worth sitting with, given
that SZZ is a diff-based method by construction.

**The finding the paper itself labels most surprising.** Sweeping the binary-search threshold:

| Threshold | Precision | Recall | F1 | Cost / fix commit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 0.76 | 0.75 | 0.76 | $0.68 |
| 32 | 0.81 | 0.80 | 0.81 | $0.67 |
| 128 | 0.80 | 0.79 | 0.80 | $0.39 |
| 512 | 0.81 | 0.80 | 0.81 | $0.35 |
| ∞ (no binary search) | 0.82 | 0.81 | **0.82** | **$0.33** |

The paper's own contribution is dominated by its removal — better F1 at roughly half the cost, the abstract's
**43 % token-cost reduction**.

**Simple-SZZ-Agent** then beats SZZ-Agent on three of four datasets: **0.81 vs 0.77**, 0.75 vs 0.75,
**0.75 vs 0.67**, **0.65 vs 0.60**.

**How the agent actually works, measured rather than asserted.** Mean tool calls per run: Read 9.8, Grep 2.7,
Write 1.0, Glob 0.8. Grep patterns originate from the fix's removed lines (**48.1 %**) and its commit message
(**43.6 %**). Across 243 grep calls the median pattern is 21 characters and **63.8 % are plain strings, not
regexes**. The authors note the removed-lines case is SZZ's own move performed with grep instead of
`git blame` — and the worked pure-addition example (3 insertions, 0 deletions) is one SZZ cannot attempt.
Scaling is close to flat: F1 stays above 0.80 up to 1,000 candidates, falling to **0.72** beyond that, with
cost essentially uncorrelated with candidate count ($r = -0.03$).

**Error analysis, 100 commits.** 87 correct. Of 13 failures: 4 where the true commit was not in the candidate
set, 9 selection errors — 3 faulty analysis, 2 near misses within three positions, **3 where the authors judge
the ground-truth label itself questionable**, 1 undetermined.

**Frameworks and models.** Best configuration is OpenHands with claude-sonnet-4-5 at **0.86 F1**; the cheapest
respectable one is OpenHands with minimax-m2.5 at 0.79 F1 for **$0.04** per fix commit. The authors immediately
caveat their own best number: the OpenHands runs consume three to four times more tokens, so the advantage may
be spend rather than architecture.

### Reading it critically

The appendix is where the honest reader should go. Both agents only ever search files the fix touched, so when
the bug-introducing commit lives in an untouched file they are **N/A by construction** — 5.5 % to 13.2 % of
cases depending on dataset, counted as failures in the headline tables. On the function-level-overlap split,
Simple-SZZ-Agent drops from 0.89 to 0.75 on Linux and from 0.86 to 0.52 on the Java set. The performance is
real, but it is concentrated where the fix and the bug touch the same code, and the authors say so.

Two internal inconsistencies survive into the camera-ready: the binary-search threshold is given as 33 in the
text and figure but swept as 32 in the table, and the Java dataset is described as 75 samples while the
appendix tables sum to 73. Neither affects the argument.

The threats section is unusually self-implicating. The authors apply the first author's own prior critique of
vulnerability-detection benchmarks to this paper, concede the agents may be exploiting superficial cues, and
then make the one move that actually answers it: this benchmark is not a proxy for some other capability, it
*is* the deployment task. They decline to claim the agents understand bugs.

On the strategic point, their own framing is hard to improve on: *"To break the trend of small incremental
improvements, entirely new approaches are needed."*

**CodeMechanic: Bug-Property-Guided Program Mitigation**

**Authors:** Han Zheng, Rafaila Galanopoulou, Ilia Shumailov, Tianqi Fan, Aiden Hall, Dominik Maier, Mathias Payer

**Venue:** arXiv preprint arXiv:2608.22275, cs.SE / cs.CR, submitted 23 August 2026.

**Links.** [arXiv:2608.22275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22275) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22275) · [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22275v1).
**License: arXiv non-exclusive distribution 1.0** — not a Creative Commons class licence, so no mirrored PDF
and no figure reproduction here. **Read at full published-abstract depth**, with authorship, subject class and
licence verified against the arXiv record; section-level detail not retrieved.

### The gap being targeted

The framing is operational rather than academic. Automated testing now finds memory-corruption bugs faster
than developers can trace and repair them, which leaves a window in which a known, exploitable defect sits in
shipped code. End-to-end LLM repair agents can shorten that window — but they generate open-ended code
changes and typically validate them by replaying the proof-of-concept. The paper's charge against that oracle
is specific: it accepts patches that silence the observed crash by altering unrelated behaviour. A patch that
passes has demonstrated that this PoC no longer crashes, and nothing else. Deploying it is a gamble.

### The move: constrain the output, not the oracle

Rather than strengthen validation, CodeMechanic shrinks what the model is allowed to produce. It does not ask
for a permanent repair at all. From the crash it reconstructs the violated memory-safety property, validates
the dereferenced pointer and its buffer range, and inserts a **local fail-stop guard** immediately before the
dangerous access. When the boundary check fails, execution terminates.

The trade-off is stated rather than hidden: **availability is spent to buy security**. A potential remote code
execution becomes a controlled termination, and the window stays covered while developers do the root-cause
work and write the real fix. That reframing is the contribution — mitigation as a first-class, temporary,
deliberately incomplete artefact, distinct from repair.

Three components keep LLM error bounded: a two-dimensional static-and-dynamic context extractor, in-prompt
debugging knowledge, and stepwise validation.

### What the numbers say

On **101 real-world ARVO bugs**, CodeMechanic's *first attempt* produces **47.6 % more plausible patches** —
patches passing PoC-replay validation — than the strongest baseline, while consuming **91 % fewer tokens**.
The token figure is the one to note: a narrower task specification is dramatically cheaper than open-ended
generation, and the paper gets both the accuracy and the cost on the same side of the ledger.

A manual audit goes past the plausibility oracle the paper spent its introduction criticising: CodeMechanic
yields **3.4× to 4.3× more patches semantically equivalent to developer-written repairs**. Reporting that
separately from the plausibility rate is the right call, since plausibility is exactly the metric under
suspicion.

### Why it is worth your time

The bug-property reconstruction step is the reusable part, independent of the LLM framing: it converts a crash
into an explicit safety property, which is a well-defined artefact that other tooling can consume. The
narrower observation — that constraining the hypothesis space beats strengthening the oracle when the oracle
is fundamentally weak — generalises well past memory safety.

Two things the abstract does not settle, and which the full text would need to answer before adoption: the
runtime and code-size cost of the inserted guards, and the false-positive rate of the boundary checks on
benign inputs. A fail-stop guard that fires in production on legitimate traffic converts a latent
vulnerability into an immediate outage.

**Execution-Anchored Hallucination Calibration Reranking for Verilog Code Generation**

**Authors:** Guang Yang, Xing Hu, Xiang Chen, Terry Yue Zhuo, Xin Xia

**Venue:** arXiv preprint arXiv:2608.22938, cs.SE / cs.AR, submitted 24 August 2026.

**Links.** [arXiv:2608.22938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22938) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22938) · [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22938v1).
**License: CC BY 4.0** per the arXiv licence record. **Read at full published-abstract depth**; authorship,
subject class and licence verified against the arXiv record. Section-level detail and evaluation tables not
retrieved — the numeric claims below are the ones the abstract states.

### The setting

LLMs generate code well and Verilog badly, because hardware description languages are low-resource. Sampling
many candidates raises the chance that a correct one exists; it does nothing to tell you which. Reranking is
therefore the whole game, and the paper's contribution is an empirical study of why the two obvious rerankers
both fail, followed by an architecture derived from that failure.

### The empirical finding, which is the real contribution

Across **nine models and two benchmarks**, two limitations are isolated:

**Execution-based reranking** — accept or reject on testbench pass/fail — transfers poorly across domains,
because the generated testbenches are themselves low quality. The signal is deterministic but its coverage is
bounded by an artefact that is as hard to synthesise as the code under test.

**LLM-as-a-Judge** suffers reasoning hallucination: it returns inconsistent verdicts on code that is
*execution-equivalent*. Two implementations that behave identically receive different scores, which is
disqualifying for a ranker.

The paper's framing of what to do about it is the part worth stealing: these are **two signal types with
orthogonal errors** — execution deterministic but coverage-limited, reasoning semantically rich but
hallucination-prone. Orthogonal errors are the textbook precondition for ensembling, so combine them.

### The negative result inside the positive one

The obvious combination is to let the reasoning model observe the execution results. The authors tried it and
report that it fails, for an interesting reason: the reasoner's judgements simply become **anchored on the
test outcomes**. Conditioning one signal on the other does not fuse them, it collapses them — you have
re-implemented execution-based reranking with extra steps and lost the independent semantic channel entirely.

The design follows directly: acquire the two signals **independently**, and fuse only at the decision stage.
This is late fusion justified by a measured failure of early fusion, not by convention.

**EAHC** implements it as a dual channel: **EAHC-R**, a 4B reasoning discriminator, and **EAHC-T**, a testbench
generator using retrieval-augmented generation for execution verification. The 4B discriminator is notable —
the semantic channel does not need a frontier model once it is not also being asked to simulate execution.

### Why it is worth your time

The transferable lesson is about pipeline architecture rather than Verilog. Any time two validators are
available and one is cheap-and-brittle while the other is rich-and-unreliable, the instinct is to let the
smart one look at the cheap one's output. This paper is evidence that the instinct is wrong, and that
independence is a property worth protecting even at the cost of a less informed judge.

Being a preprint at abstract depth, the ablation isolating the fusion-stage choice — and the size of the gain
over each channel alone — is exactly what a full read should check first.

judge is trusted to simulate mentally.

**Learning from the Test: Self-Referential Differential Testing for Deep RL Agents**

**Authors:** Jiahao He, Jiawei Shi, Zhou Yang, Mingyue Cheng, David Lo

**Venue:** arXiv preprint arXiv:2608.22284, 2026.

**Links.** [arXiv:2608.22284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22284) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22284).
**Evidence base: Scholar-alert abstract only** — `web_fetch` was rate-limited before this record could be
retrieved. Licence not determined.

Deep RL systems are being deployed into real decision-making, and testing them is genuinely hard for a reason
that is structural rather than incidental: differential testing needs two implementations that should agree,
and for a trained policy there is no reference implementation to compare against. Metamorphic testing needs
relations someone must state. Coverage means little when the artefact is a weight matrix.

The proposed way out is in the title — **self-referential** differential testing, deriving the comparison from
the agent itself rather than from an external reference. If that works it is a neat inversion, because the
absence of a second implementation is exactly what made the technique inapplicable.

The design question a full read must answer is what plays the role of the second implementation, and whether
the disagreements it surfaces are genuine faults or merely the ordinary stochasticity of a policy. A
differential tester whose alarms are dominated by seed variance is not useful, and for RL that is the default
outcome unless the construction is careful.

Flagged for the methodology rather than the domain: a working recipe for differential testing without a
reference oracle would transfer well beyond RL, to any learned component embedded in a larger system.

**Towards LLM-Enhanced Android Taint Analysis**

**Authors:** Nicolò Miazzo, Marco Alecci, Jordan Samhi, Jacques Klein, Eleonora Losiouk

**Venue:** arXiv preprint arXiv:2608.24269, 2026.

**Links.** [arXiv:2608.24269](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24269) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.24269).
**Evidence base: Scholar-alert abstract only** — retrieval blocked by rate limiting. Licence not determined.

Taint analysis for Android has a well-understood ceiling, and the abstract names it correctly: the obstacle is
not the dataflow algorithm but the difficulty of accurately *modelling the Android framework* — lifecycle
callbacks, ICC, reflection, native boundaries, and the sheer surface area of the SDK. FlowDroid's imprecision
is largely inherited from an incomplete model of an enormous platform, not from the analysis engine.

The word doing the work in the title is **enhanced**. This is positioned as augmentation of a mature static
analyser rather than replacement of it by a model, which is the correct architecture and, encouragingly, is
becoming the default in this line of work. The interesting engineering question is where the seam is placed:
using an LLM to synthesise or complete framework models is a promising and checkable use, since a proposed
model can be validated against runtime behaviour; using one to decide whether a flow is a real leak is not,
since there is nothing to check it against.

The Luxembourg group (Samhi, Klein) has consistently produced reusable Android analysis artefacts, which
raises the prior that the framework-modelling contribution is usable independently of the LLM framing. That
detachability is the reason to read it — the model-completion component may be worth more than the end-to-end
result.

Relevant to a standing interest here: static-analysis papers have been positively rated in this digest before,
and this is the window's only static-analysis entry.

**FlowScout: From Execution Feedback to Reliable Tool-Using Agent Workflows**

**Authors:** Shuang Hao, Yifan Lu, Bihuan Chen, Xin Peng

**Venue:** arXiv preprint arXiv:2608.10039, 2026.

**Links.** [arXiv:2608.10039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10039) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10039).
**Evidence base: Scholar-alert abstract only.** Licence not determined. *Recovered by the 7-day liveness sweep —
this thread arrived 27 August and had never been triaged by any run.*

Agentic workflows — LLMs, tools and control logic arranged into an explicit execution structure — have become
the standard abstraction for building reliable automation, and the abstract's complaint is that *constructing*
a good one is still manual. FlowScout's proposal is to derive the structure from execution feedback rather
than from a designer's guess.

This is the same shape as the window's central finding. Risse and Böhme (1.1) discovered by ablation that
their hand-designed two-stage structure was worse than none at all; FlowScout starts from the premise that
hand-designed structure is the problem and tries to learn it from observed behaviour. Both are reacting to the
fact that agent scaffolding is currently written on intuition and rarely ablated.

What to check on a full read: whether the learned workflows are actually more reliable than a
single-agent-with-tools baseline. That baseline is the one 1.1 shows to be surprisingly strong, and it is the
comparison this literature most often omits.

**DOCSCHISEL: Adaptive Tool Documentation Optimization Framework for LLM Agents**

**Authors:** Yifan Lu, Kaiyuan Zhang, Bihuan Chen, Xin Peng

**Venue:** arXiv preprint arXiv:2608.10037, 2026.

Companion piece to FlowScout from the same lab, and the pairing is the interesting part. Where 1.9 optimises
the control structure, this optimises the *grounding resource*: tool documentation. The abstract's framing is
that existing work concentrates on improving the agent's tool-use capability and treats the documentation as
fixed, when the documentation is a critical part of what the agent is reasoning from.

That is the same insight this digest recorded on 2026-08-30 in a different domain — the ISSTA property-based
testing work found its entire gain in annotating widgets before generating anything, with an 18-point accuracy
swing attributable to grounding rather than generation. Two labs, two domains, one conclusion: the leverage is
in the artefact the model reads, not the model or the prompt.

The evaluation question is whether the optimised documentation transfers across models and agent frameworks,
or whether it is being fitted to one model's quirks. Documentation tuned into a per-model artefact is much
less useful than documentation that is simply better.

Learning-based malware detection is a crowded area and most entries in it are screened out of this digest.
This one is retained on two independent signals: TSE, and a problem statement about *robustness* rather than
about another detector. The two challenges named — concept drift causing model aging, and code obfuscation as
an adversarial input transformation — are the two reasons deployed detectors decay, and they are usually
studied separately.

Treating them jointly is the potentially novel part, because the natural defences pull against each other:
adapting continuously to drift makes a model easier to poison, while hardening against obfuscation tends to
mean committing to features that drift. A paper that handles both without one undoing the other would be
worth the read; one that stacks two independent mitigations would not. The abstract does not distinguish these

**ASFuzz: Detecting Linux Kernel Asynchronous Concurrency Bugs via Asynchronous Shadow Fuzzing**

**Authors:** Jian Zhao, Yifan Geng, Jian Wang, Yi Chen, Hao Chen, Yu Wang, et al.

**Venue:** IEEE Transactions, 2026.

**Links.** [IEEE Xplore 11660760](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/11660760/) · [Scholar lookup](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=ASFuzz+Detecting+Linux+Kernel+Asynchronous+Concurrency+Bugs).
**Evidence base: Scholar-alert abstract only.** Licence not determined. *Recovered by the 7-day liveness sweep.*

The technical observation is sharp enough to be worth recording even without a full read: concurrency bugs
arising from *asynchronous-to-synchronous* interactions are hard to fuzz because the asynchronous task fires
on a delay, so the crash is separated in time from the input that caused it. Conventional kernel fuzzers
attribute crashes to the syscall sequence that immediately preceded them, and that attribution is simply wrong
for this bug class — which means the bugs are not merely rare in the search, they are systematically
mis-credited when found.

"Asynchronous shadow fuzzing" suggests running a shadow execution to make the delayed interaction observable.
If the mechanism generalises past the kernel it applies to any event-driven system with deferred callbacks,
which is most of them.

Borderline only because it was not deep-read and the venue string from the alert is incomplete. Promote if
kernel concurrency is on your critical path.

## Cross-Paper Synthesis

### The oracle is the bottleneck, and three groups said so independently

The papers in this window were not written together, but four of them are arguing about the same thing: what
it means for machine-generated code to be *validated*, and whether the check we are using establishes what we
think it does.

The chain is worth laying out because each link fails differently.

**Pass-the-PoC is too weak.** CodeMechanic (1.2) shows the failure directly: a repair agent can make the
crash go away by changing behaviour unrelated to the bug, and PoC replay will sign it off. The response is
notable for what it *isn't* — no attempt to build a stronger validator. Instead the hypothesis space is
narrowed until a weak oracle suffices: reconstruct the violated safety property, emit a fail-stop guard, and
accept a controlled termination in place of a repair. The token cost falls by 91 % because a constrained task
is a cheaper task, and the audit finding of 3.4×–4.3× more developer-equivalent patches is what tells you the
constraint did not merely trade one kind of wrongness for another.

**Pass-the-tests is too weak in the other direction.** The COLM red-teaming paper (1.4) takes the same oracle
and shows it admits patches that are actively harmful rather than merely irrelevant. Together with 1.2 this
brackets the problem: the oracle used across the APR literature is permissive in both the benign and the
adversarial direction, and neither failure is visible in the metric the field reports.

**So use two oracles — but do not let them talk.** EAHC (1.3) is the constructive answer and contains the
window's most transferable negative result. Execution signals and reasoning signals have orthogonal errors,
which is the standard justification for ensembling them. The authors then tried the obvious implementation —
show the reasoning model the execution results — and found it collapses the ensemble, because the reasoner
anchors on the test outcome and stops contributing an independent view. Late fusion here is not a design
convention; it is a measured consequence of early fusion destroying the second signal.

**And the human oracle has the same defect.** BigCodeArena (1.5) observes that arena-style preference
collection assumes an annotator who can judge by reading, which fails for code because correctness is a
property of execution. Putting execution in front of the human is the same move EAHC makes for the model
judge. Two papers, two kinds of judge, one conclusion: do not ask a judge to simulate execution in its head.

### The scaffolding recession

The second thread cuts against a strong prior in the current literature, which is that agentic systems get
better as you add structure.

Risse and Böhme (1.1) provide the cleanest evidence to the contrary, and it is unusually credible because it
undermines their own contribution. Their engineered pipeline — SZZ candidate filtering, then agentic binary
search over file history — reaches 0.77 F1, comfortably past the 0.64 state of the art. The threshold sweep
then shows that setting the binary-search bound to infinity, which is to say removing the search, gives 0.82
F1 at half the cost. Simple-SZZ-Agent, which is a stock coding agent handed the commit history and
read/grep/glob, beats the pipeline on three of four datasets.

The mechanism is legible in their instrumentation rather than left to speculation. Roughly ten reads and three
greps per run; grep patterns drawn from the fix's removed lines 48.1 % of the time and its commit message
43.6 % of the time; nearly two-thirds of patterns plain strings rather than regexes. The agent rediscovers
SZZ's core heuristic — trace the deleted lines — and implements it with grep instead of `git blame`. Then it
does the thing SZZ structurally cannot, which is handle a fix consisting only of additions. The scaffolding
was encoding a strategy the agent would have found anyway, while constraining it from finding the others.

FlowScout (1.9) and DOCSCHISEL (1.10) approach the same suspicion from the other end: if hand-written
scaffolding is unreliable, derive the workflow from execution feedback, and invest in the documentation the
agent is grounding on. Which lands on the conclusion this digest recorded on 2026-08-30 from an entirely
different set of papers — that the leverage sits in the artefact the model reads rather than in the model or
the orchestration.

The sharpened version, after this window: **before reporting that your scaffold works, ablate it to nothing.**
The strongest paper here is strong precisely because its authors ran that ablation and published the result
that cost them their headline contribution. Two of 1.1's four datasets would have looked better if they had
not.

### A methodological note that is not about LLMs

1.1's leakage handling deserves separate mention as reusable practice: ground-truth hashes replaced throughout,
`Fixes:` tags stripped, the repository flattened into loose files so the agent cannot run `git log`, network
access disabled, and — the part most papers skip — a fresh dataset built entirely from fixes published after
the model's training cutoff, on which performance went *up* rather than down. That last step converts "we
believe there is no leakage" into a measurement. Any evaluation of an agent against historical repository
data should be held to it.

## Writing & Rationale Insights

**Piggyback on the news, then use it as the problem statement.** 1.1 opens by noting that SZZ's authors have
just won the 2026 SIGSOFT Impact Award — and turns the celebration into the indictment: twenty years, more
than 1,300 citations, ten points of F1. The award is what makes the stagnation quantifiable and the paper
timely at once. The authors state the consequence plainly: *"To break the trend of small incremental
improvements, entirely new approaches are needed."*

**Publish the ablation that kills your contribution, and label it.** The same paper marks its threshold sweep
as its most surprising finding, then restructures the entire second half of the paper around the fact that
removing its own binary search improves results. Most papers would have quietly adopted the better
configuration and presented it as the design. Making the reversal the narrative buys more credibility than
the original contribution would have, and it is the single most imitable rhetorical move in this window.

**Pre-empt the critique you are personally famous for.** 1.1's benchmark-validity paragraph applies the first
author's own prior work on flawed vulnerability-detection benchmarks to the present paper, concedes the agents
may be exploiting superficial cues, declines to claim they understand bugs — and then answers with the one
argument that actually holds: this benchmark is the deployment task, not a proxy for some other capability.
Disarming your own strongest critic works best when your strongest critic is you.

**Report the negative experiment inside the positive result.** EAHC (1.3) could have presented late fusion as
an architectural choice. Instead it reports that early fusion was tried and failed, and *why* — the reasoner
anchored on the execution outcome. The design is thereby justified by evidence rather than by taste, and the
failed variant is more useful to a reader than the successful one.

**Caveat your own best number before a reviewer does.** 1.1's strongest configuration is flagged by the
authors themselves as possibly an artefact of three-to-four-times higher token consumption rather than of
architecture. Volunteering the confound costs a small amount of headline strength and buys the reader's trust
in every other number in the table.

**Name the trade you are making instead of burying it.** CodeMechanic (1.2) states outright that it spends
availability to buy security — a fail-stop guard turns potential remote code execution into a crash. Framing
mitigation as explicitly temporary and explicitly incomplete makes it a defensible artefact rather than a weak
repair, and pre-empts the obvious objection by conceding it as the design.
